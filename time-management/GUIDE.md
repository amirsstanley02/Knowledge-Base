# Guide: Cutting Overwhelm Down to Size

Concrete tactics, not motivation. Each one maps to a template in this folder.

## 1. Get it out of your head (Capture)

As long as tasks live only in your memory, your brain keeps re-checking all of them,
all the time — that's most of what "overwhelmed" *feels* like. Write every open loop
into `templates/inbox.md` the moment it appears: an ask in a meeting, an email,
a "we should probably..." thought at 11pm. One list, not five apps.

## 2. Triage ruthlessly (the 4 D's)

Every item in the inbox gets exactly one label, using `templates/priority-matrix.md`:

- **Do** — urgent + important. Only things that truly must happen this week.
- **Schedule** — important, not urgent. Give it a specific date, not "soon."
- **Delegate** — someone else can do this, even if not as well as you. Hand it off.
- **Drop** — nobody will notice if this never happens. Cross it out and move on.

Most overwhelm comes from treating everything as "Do." Force a number: if more than
5-7 items land in "Do" for the week, something is being under-triaged — push harder
into Schedule/Delegate/Drop.

## 3. Say no without guilt

A few default responses that work in most work contexts:

- "I can take this on if [X] comes off my plate — which should I drop?"
- "I can get to this by [specific date], not sooner. Does that work?"
- "That's not something I can own well right now — can [name] or [team] take it?"
- Simply: "I don't have capacity for this right now."

Saying yes to a new task is implicitly saying no to something already on your list
(often your free time). Make that trade-off explicit out loud, especially to whoever
is assigning the work.

## 4. Batch and time-box

- Group similar small tasks (emails, approvals, quick replies) into 1-2 fixed
  windows a day instead of reacting to each one as it lands.
- Give every task a time box *before* starting it (e.g. "45 min for this doc").
  Work expands to fill the time available — capping it forces triage of scope too.
- Turn off notifications outside your batch windows where possible.

## 5. Protect free time like a deadline

Free time that isn't scheduled loses to whatever is scheduled. In
`templates/weekly-plan.md`, block your free time **first**, before filling in work
tasks, and treat it as immovable as a client meeting. If something threatens to
eat it, that's a signal to delegate or drop something else — not to cancel the block.

## 6. Review weekly, not just daily

Once a week (see `templates/weekly-plan.md`), step back and ask:
- What actually got done vs. planned, and why the gap?
- What's recurring that could be automated, templated, or delegated permanently?
- Is the "Do" pile shrinking over time, or just refilling? If it's just refilling,
  the leak is upstream — new commitments are being taken on faster than they're
  being finished. Fix that at the source (say no more, more often).

## 7. When it's still too much

If after honest triage the "Do" list still doesn't fit in the week:
1. Tell whoever assigned the work, specifically, what will slip and by when —
   before the deadline, not after.
2. Ask directly: "given everything on my plate, what's the actual priority order?"
   Let the person with the fuller picture make the trade-off explicit.
3. Treat "I have too much to do" as data to escalate, not a personal failing to
   push through silently.
